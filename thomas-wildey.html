About Thomas Wildey "The Founder"

Thomas Wildey was born in London, England in 1782 and trained as a coach spring maker before becoming a member of the Odd Fellows in his homeland. Seeking better opportunities, he emigrated to the United States in 1817 and settled in Baltimore, Maryland. Finding that Odd Fellowship had no stable footing in America, Wildey took it upon himself to revive and establish the Order. On April 26, 1819, he gathered a small group at a tavern in Baltimore and founded the first lodge, marking the beginning of the Independent Order of Odd Fellows in the United States. From these humble beginnings, Wildey guided the growth of the Order into a widespread brotherhood grounded inmutual aid, charity, and moral development. His leadership emphasized caring for the sick, supporting the needy, and uplifting communities — principles that defined Odd Fellowship during a time when noformal social safety nets existed.

Wildey is remembered not only as the founder but as the driving force behind the early expansion and structure of the Order. He helped shape its system of lodges, degrees, and charitable mission, eventually leading to the independence of the American branch from its English roots. By the time of his death in 1861, Odd Fellowship had grown into a major institution with thousands of members devoted to the ideals of Friendship, Love, and Truth. His legacy continues to live on in every lodge, where his vision of brotherhood and service remains central. More than an organizer, Wildey embodied the spirit of an Odd Fellow — a man committed to unity, compassion, and the betterment of society.

Among the beliefs and sentiments attributed to Thomas Wildey are reflections that capture the heart of Odd Fellowship. He strongly upheld the idea that the Order exists to “visit the sick, relieve thedistressed, bury the dead, and educate the orphan,” a guiding principle that became one of the most recognized teachings of the fraternity. He also believed deeply in the power of unity and mutual support, often emphasizing that men, regardless of their background or trade, could come together in brotherhood for a common good. His life and work reflect a quiet but powerful conviction: that true fraternity is measured not by words or status, but by acts of charity, loyalty, and service to others.
