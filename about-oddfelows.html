About Odd Fellows

The Independent Order of Odd Fellows traces its origins to England during the 17th and 18th centuries, a time when society was structured around trade guilds that limited membership to specific professions. In contrast, certain groups began accepting members from different trades, which was considered unusual; thus, they were called “Odd Fellows,” meaning individuals who did not conform to the customary order. Far from implying strangeness, the term “odd” signified uniqueness and inclusivity. These early fraternities gradually developed into organized lodges, particularly under influential branches such as the Manchester Unity, and were primarily devoted to mutual aid — providing financial and social support to members during times of sickness, death, or hardship, long before modern welfare systems existed.

The movement expanded significantly in the early 19th century when it reached the United States. In 1819, the Order was formally established in Baltimore through the efforts of Thomas Wildey, who is widely regarded as the founder of American Odd Fellowship. Under his leadership, the organization grew rapidly and became one of the most prominent fraternal societies in the world, extending its influence across continents, including the Philippines. During its height, the Order was instrumental in building orphanages, homes for the elderly, and hospitals, reflecting its enduring commitment to charity and community welfare. Its teachings were expressed through enduring symbols such as the Three Links — representing Friendship, Love, and Truth — and the Heart in Hand, symbolizing compassion and benevolence.

Despite its well-documented history, Odd Fellowship has also been surrounded by various myths and misconceptions. Some claim that it has ancient or secret origins linked to groups like the Knights Templar, or that its rituals possess hidden mystical power. However, there is no historical evidence to support such claims. Its ceremonies and symbols are not supernatural but are instead moral and allegorical in nature, designed to teach values, strengthen brotherhood, and guide members toward personal development. Likewise, the belief that the Order is an elite or secretive society misrepresents its true character, as it was historically rooted among ordinary working people seeking mutual support and moral improvement.

Ultimately, the Odd Fellows embody both a practical and philosophical purpose. Historically, they provided essential social support in times when such systems were absent; symbolically, they represent a journey toward self-betterment and service to others. In this sense, an Odd Fellow is not merely “odd” in name but is a traveler in a deeper sense — one who continually strives toward truth, charity, and a more meaningful life.
